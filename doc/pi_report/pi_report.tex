\textbf{Key results}
\begin{itemize}
\item Half-hourly electricity demand of an NHS site can be forecast a day ahead with a normalised error of \textbf{11.5\,\%}, against 18.5\,\% for seasonal naive. The gain holds on 99\,\% of sites.
\item \textbf{Foundation models used without any training on NHS data lead.} Transformer neural processes trained on the panel itself reach 13.7\,\%, behind LightGBM at 13.1\,\%. Building descriptors from the estates returns can replace a site's identity in LightGBM without loss.
\item Known-ahead covariates improve every model family by about 0.7 points, and \textbf{forecast weather is as good as perfect weather} at this range. Reconciling across the hierarchy lowers the national error from 8.0 to \textbf{6.8\,\%}.
\item \textbf{24 sites with on-site CHP carry 70\,\% of demand.} Their meters record grid import, which switches with the CHP: day-to-day variability is 36\,\% against 10\,\% at acute hospitals without CHP, and forecast error 16.2\,\% against 3.8\,\%.
\item \textbf{Demand responds nonlinearly to temperature.} It is flat for daily means between 14 and 20\,$^\circ$C, 25 to 32\,\% higher below $-2$\,$^\circ$C, and rises ever faster with heat: 8 to 10\,\% on days whose maximum reaches 30\,$^\circ$C and 14 to 18\,\% at 32\,$^\circ$C and above. Hot days move demand into the evening: $+23$\,\% at 19:00.
\item The electricity record contains \textbf{85 extreme-weather events} at national scale. Cold spells raise demand by a median 6.7\,\%, heatwaves by 4.2\,\%, and at inpatient sites a heatwave's third day costs 2.6\,\% more than its temperature alone.
\item \textbf{The weather effects hold up as quasi-experiments.} With every national and regional day shock removed, a degree of cold still adds 1.1\,\% and a degree of heat 1.5 to 2\,\% (about 3\,\% per degree of a regionally hot day); reassigning weather between sites at random produces such effects 0 and 6 times in 200, and an independent thermometer, the nearest Met Office station, gives the same slopes. Days reaching 30\,$^\circ$C carry no extra demand beyond their temperature.
\item \textbf{The average slope hides the sites.} Every site's own cold and heat slope, with sampling noise removed, shows real variation: the cold slope runs from about zero to $+3$\,\% per $^\circ$C across sites, the heat slope from $-4$ to $+7$, with 39 sites that fall on hot days against 48 that rise. Cold has a structure, and it is the heating fuel: acute hospitals, which heat with gas, barely respond ($+0.05$\,\% per $^\circ$C), every other building use adds 1.2 to 1.4 points, and a one-SD higher gas-to-electricity ratio removes 0.45. Nothing in the estates returns explains the heat slope.
\item The estates returns confirm the annual meter totals of 65 sites exactly. Replacing gas heating with heat pumps would push \textbf{48\,\% of 631 gas-heated English sites}, and 68\,\% of acute hospitals, above their grid connection.
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Data}
The Energy Systems Catapult extract covers 284 NHS sites. Half the sites have half-hourly electricity metering, and a small group of large acute hospitals dominates total demand.

\section{Experiment}
The forecasting target is the half-hourly electricity demand of 91 NHS sites in England, those with at least 85\,\% of readings observed and a year of history. Each forecast is issued at 16:30 local time and covers the 48 half-hours of the following day. The test window runs from June 2025 to May 2026 with 69 forecast days that rotate through the weekdays, giving 6,279 site-days.

Scores use observed readings only. nMAE is the mean absolute error divided by the site's mean demand. CRPS, in kWh per half hour, scores the whole predictive distribution on nine quantiles. Coverage is the share of observations inside the nominal 80\,\% interval. Skill is the relative improvement over seasonal naive, the value one week earlier.

\section{Accuracy at site level}
LightGBM and the five foundation models improve on seasonal naive by 38 to 48\,\% in CRPS. Chronos-2 and TimesFM~3.0 lead with demand history alone; T0-beta sits with TimesFM and TiRex-2 just behind LightGBM with weather. The covariates lift every family, TabPFN-TS most because its own features carry no weather, and TiRex-2 and T0-beta least. SARIMA, refitted at every forecast day, is no more accurate than naive on the median site: it does well on large regular sites and fails on small erratic ones.

Transformer neural processes (TNP, pseudo-token TNP and gridded TNP), trained across sites and forecast days of the panel's training years, reach 13.7 to 14.6\,\% nMAE. That places them behind LightGBM and every foundation model, with intervals that are too wide (85 to 87\,\% coverage), and covariates do not improve them. Replacing LightGBM's site identifier with ERIC building descriptors (floor area, age, CHP, heat pumps, energy intensity) keeps its accuracy and improves CRPS from 6.27 to 6.15. That is a route to forecasting sites with no meter history.

\section{Probabilistic quality and the weather ensemble}
TimesFM~3.0 and T0-beta are calibrated, with 79 to 81\,\% of observations inside their 80\,\% intervals, and TiRex-2 is slightly wide at 83\,\%. Chronos-2 and TabPFN-TS are slightly narrow at 75 to 77\,\%, and LightGBM is narrow at 73\,\%.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/ensemble}
\captionof{table}{Chronos-2 with the same covariates and different weather inputs.}
\end{figblock}

Running Chronos-2 once per ensemble member and mixing the 51 resulting distributions gives the same scores as using the ensemble mean or the control member. Member forecasts differ by 0.6\,\% of mean demand on the median site-day, far less than the width of the model's own interval. The spread is nonetheless informative: the error of the forecast rises from 3\,\% to 30\,\% of mean demand between the lowest and highest tenth of spread.

\section{When and where errors occur}
Errors are largest on the working-day plateau, between 09:00 and 16:00, and smallest at night. Skill is positive almost everywhere on the map and is highest at the large acute sites. Regional totals range from 2.8\,\% nMAE in the East of England to 15.5\,\% in the South West. The two weakest regions are dominated by large CHP sites, Royal Devon \& Exeter with 64\,\% of the South West and Royal Preston and North Manchester in the North West, whose recorded demand halves on some days.

\paragraph{Hot days.} Scored by the kind of target day (ERA5 daily maximum at or above 28\,$^\circ$C, 214 site-days; 25--28, 234; other June--September, 1816; daily mean at or below 2\,$^\circ$C, 73), every model errs about half a point more on the hottest days than on other summer days and three to four points more on the coldest. It is not the weather forecast: LightGBM with realised ERA5 weather is no better on 28\,$^\circ$C days than with the IFS forecast (12.8 against 12.8\,\% nMAE), while without weather it loses 1.4 points. The gap is in how the models use heat; Section~\ref{sec:weather} prescribes the features (daily maximum and evening temperature, station-corrected maxima, the day's solar radiation, the school and Christmas calendar). A LightGBM variant carrying those features (daily forecast maximum, minimum and evening temperature, station-corrected hot-day flags, solar radiation, the school and Christmas calendar and the distance to a bank holiday) does not move the hottest days (12.8 against 12.8\,\%) and gains half a point on the coldest; the hot-day penalty is plant switching that no day-ahead feature carries.

\section{Hierarchy and reconciliation}
The data form an exact hierarchy: meters sum to sites, sites to trusts, trusts to NHS regions and regions to the national total of the 91 sites. Forecasting each level directly becomes more accurate with aggregation.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/hierarchy_direct}
\captionof{table}{nMAE (\%) of direct forecasts at each level.}
\end{figblock}

Direct forecasts at different levels do not add up. Reconciliation maps them to a coherent set. Eight approaches were compared on the same 61 forecast days, with the variance-based approaches learning only from errors on earlier days.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/reconciliation}
\captionof{table}{nMAE (\%) after reconciliation of the Chronos-2 forecasts with forecast weather. Best coherent value per level in bold. The same ranking holds for TimesFM~3.0 and LightGBM.}
\end{figblock}

Weighted least squares with variance scaling keeps the accuracy of the meter and site forecasts and gives the best trust, regional and national figures. Summing site forecasts is nearly as good. Approaches that ignore the very different sizes of the series, or that split a total by recent shares, make the meter and site forecasts two to four times worse. The full-covariance method is held back by the short error history available for 218 series.

Sites were grouped by the shape of their average weekday and weekend profile. Apart from two sites that use two to three times more electricity at night than by day, there are three shapes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Function sets the shape, not the type of trust.} Mental health, acute and community trusts all have sites in every group. Inpatient community hospitals are round-the-clock; administration buildings, treatment centres and outpatient departments follow the working day.
\item \textbf{Shape follows size.} Round-the-clock sites are the largest and office-like sites the smallest, and the latter vary twice as much from day to day.
\item \textbf{Trusts form two branches}: eight with a marked working-day cycle and thirteen with flatter profiles.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sites with on-site CHP}
Twenty-four sites, most of them general acute hospitals, run gas-fired combined heat and power. Their meters record grid import, which is the building's demand minus the CHP's output. The CHP runs, trips, is serviced or is turned down for reasons unrelated to the building, so the series carries a second, hidden driver. York Hospital, for example, imported more than 1.8 times its median on 95 weekdays between June 2024 and May 2025, most of them from January to May 2025.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{tables/chp}}
\captionof{table}{Sites with on-site CHP against acute hospitals without CHP and all other sites; medians per group. Load factor is mean over 99th-percentile import. Jump days are weekdays more than 30\,\% above the median of the surrounding 21 weekdays. nMAE and CRPS skill are for Chronos-2 with forecast weather on the scored sites. The last column is the change in demand per cooling degree day (Section~\ref{sec:weather}).}
\end{figblock}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Acute hospitals without CHP are the most predictable sites} in the panel (3.8\,\% nMAE). Skill against seasonal naive is the same in every group, so the extra error at CHP sites comes from CHP switching that weather and calendar cannot predict.
\item \textbf{CHP sites cannot be checked against the estates returns}: their annual meter-to-ERIC ratio spans 0.39 to 1.08 (10th to 90th percentile), against 0.90 to 1.10 elsewhere.
\item \textbf{CHP drives the totals}: trust, regional and national series, and their year-on-year swings, largely follow CHP operation. Half-hourly CHP generation would turn import back into demand.
\item \textbf{ERIC 2024/25 shows what sits behind the meter.} The 2024/25 return (published October 2025, inside our sample) adds each site's CHP electrical output, exports and unit size. On the 20 CHP sites with a plausible return, implied demand (import + CHP output $-$ exports) is 23.3\,MW against 9.2\,MW of grid import: the meters see 40\,\% of what these sites use. CHP covers a median 50\,\% of a site's electricity (up to 78\,\% at Royal Preston) at a median capacity factor of 0.70. Our metered import matches the returned import at most sites (0.95--1.06) and names the partial supplies: Wythenshawe (27\,\%) and Great Western (2\,\%).
\end{itemize}

\section{Weather and demand}\label{sec:weather}
\subsection{Response to temperature}
Daily demand at each site is compared with a 14--16\,$^\circ$C day of the same month at the same site, after removing day-of-week, bank-holiday, Christmas and school-holiday effects. The comparison uses 140,326 site-days, December 2022 to May 2026, with temperature in 2\,$^\circ$C bands so that the shape of the response is not assumed. Intervals are 95\,\%, clustered by week.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/temperature_response}
\captionof{table}{Change in daily electricity demand (\%) against a 14--16\,$^\circ$C day of the same month, with 95\,\% intervals. Number of sites in brackets. Inpatient and outpatient groups exclude CHP sites.}
\end{figblock}

\begin{figblock}
\pair{temperature_response_curve}{temperature_response_hourly}
\captionof{figure}{Left: change in daily demand against daily mean temperature, by site group, with 95\,\% intervals; the open-ended bands are drawn at $-3$ and 25\,$^\circ$C. Right: the same for each hour of the day at inpatient sites without CHP.}
\end{figblock}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A hockey stick, not a line.} Demand is flat between 14 and 20\,$^\circ$C and rises by 1.5 to 2\,\% per degree below that. Above 20\,$^\circ$C it rises ever faster: $+2.6$, $+6.3$ and $+10.0$\,\% in successive bands at inpatient sites. A single cooling-degree-day slope understates the hot tail, and a day below $-2$\,$^\circ$C still costs 2.5 times as much as a day above 24\,$^\circ$C.
\item \textbf{Hot days move demand into the evening.} On days above 24\,$^\circ$C inpatient demand at 19:00 is 23\,\% higher, while midday hours change little; on 22--24\,$^\circ$C days they are 8 to 9\,\% lower. Cold days lift every hour by a similar 22 to 29\,\%. The evening shift lands on the 17:00--20:00 system peak.
\item \textbf{Heat accumulates.} Beyond its own temperature, the second hot day adds 2.1\,\% and the third and later 2.6\,\% at inpatient sites; long cold spells add 2.5 to 3.0\,\% from the third day.
\item \textbf{Other weather}: wind on cold days adds 0.4\,\% per m/s, sunshine lowers demand by 1.0 to 1.6\,\% per kWh/m$^2$ (daylight), and humidity adds nothing beyond temperature.
\item \textbf{Extreme heat.} On the daily maximum, demand is flat up to 24\,$^\circ$C and then rises by a growing step per band: at inpatient sites $+1.1$\,\% at 24--26\,$^\circ$C, $+2.8$ at 26--28, $+5.3$ at 28--30, $+7.6$ at 30--32 and $+14.2$\,\% [9.9, 18.6] at 32\,$^\circ$C and above; outpatient sites $+10.1$ and $+17.8$\,\%, with daily peaks up 27\,\%. The hottest band rests on 16 inpatient site-days on four dates in 2023--2025.
\item \textbf{CHP sites are the mirror image}: flat on cold days, when the CHP runs for heat, and 27 to 32\,\% higher on warm days, when it turns down.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extreme-weather events}
Events were catalogued from hourly ERA5 at the 275 located sites with official definitions where they exist. An event counts at national scale when at least 5\,\% of sites are affected at once. The catalogue recovers the named storms of 2023--2025, Storm Babet's rain, the four heatwaves of summer 2025 and the cold spells of December 2022, January 2024 and January 2025. The record-breaking heat of late May 2026, 35.1\,$^\circ$C at Kew, falls just after the electricity record ends.

\subsection{Causal estimates}
Weather is assigned by nature, but exposure is not: southern and urban sites run warmer and are more often acute hospitals. Every design here absorbs that with site-level fixed effects and differs in which time-varying confounders it removes. Two doses, the two arms of the hockey stick: degrees of daily mean below 14\,$^\circ$C (cold) and above 20\,$^\circ$C (heat). Sites without CHP; 95\,\% intervals two-way clustered by site and date.

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/causal_specs}
\captionof{table}{Effect of a degree of cold and of heat on daily demand (\%) under nested fixed effects. A: site $\times$ month, site $\times$ weekday and calendar controls; B: with date effects, so only the day's cross-site temperature gradient identifies the effect; C: region $\times$ date effects; D (``B + weather''): B with the day's sunshine, wind and rain. SD is the dose variation left after the fixed effects.}
\end{figblock}

Date effects remove everything common to all sites on a day; what remains is how much warmer or colder one site was than the others, relative to its own month: a difference-in-differences with a continuous dose. They also remove 94\,\% of the identifying variation, because the panel is one weather system. The cold effect is unchanged by them; the heat effect halves and loses precision, then recovers once sunshine is held fixed, since hot days are sunny and sunshine lowers lighting demand.

\begin{figblock}

\end{figblock}

\begin{figblock}
\pair[0.9]{causal_specs}{causal_event_study}

\begin{figblock}\centering\tablefont
\input{tables/causal_events}
\captionof{table}{Stacked event studies. Treated sites are inside a site-level event, controls have none within ten days; the temperature column is how much hotter or colder the treated sites were on event days, demand the matching difference in demand, and the last columns the ratio and the $p$-value of the pre-event coefficients.}
\end{figblock}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No anticipation, no rebound.} Pre-event coefficients are flat ($p = 0.16$ and $0.71$) and demand returns to its baseline after the event. The design's weakness is its dose: during a national event the clean controls are still hot or cold, only 1.1 to 1.2\,$^\circ$C less so, so the expected gap of 1.5 to 2\,\% sits inside intervals several times wider than the fixed-effect estimates'. The point estimates agree with the table above.
\item \textbf{Randomisation.} Reassigning whole weather series between sites at random, 200 times, yields a cold effect as large as the observed one 0 times and a heat effect as large 6 times.
\item \textbf{Leads.} Tomorrow's temperature, given today's and the past week's, carries a small heat coefficient ($+0.45$\,\% per $^\circ$C, jointly $p = 0.02$) and nothing for cold. It survives a control for the same evening's temperature, so it is either anticipation (heat-health alerts go out a day or two ahead) or measurement error in the site's grid-point temperature letting adjacent days proxy for the true value. The lag structure is otherwise clean: largest the same day, carrying into the next, nil after that.
\item \textbf{Extreme days.} A day whose maximum reaches 30\,$^\circ$C carries $-1.5$\,\% $[-4.3, +1.3]$ beyond what its mean temperature implies: no extra kick, if anything a shortfall against a linear extrapolation, which is what cooling plant at its limit would look like. Demand keeps rising through 30\,$^\circ$C at about 1.2\,\% per degree of daily maximum.
\item \textbf{Space.} Temperature anomalies correlate 0.998 between sites within 25\,km and 0.89 at 200 to 400\,km; after date effects the demand residuals correlate 0.01 at every distance, so no regional shock is left to confound. Given a site's own temperature, its neighbours' (30 to 150\,km) adds nothing for cold; for heat the two are too alike to split, and only their sum, about 2.8\,\% per degree of a regionally hot day, is well determined. Standard errors clustered by region $\times$ week or by ICB are no larger than the two-way ones used.
\item \textbf{The thermometer is not the problem.} Re-estimated on the 106,425 site-days where ERA5, ERA5-Land (9\,km) and the nearest Met Office station (NOAA ISD, median 13.5\,km, hourly) all report, the three give the same slopes: cold $+1.06$ to $+1.11$\,\% per $^\circ$C on every thermometer, heat $+1.69$ (station), $+1.97$ (ERA5) and $+2.31$ (ERA5-Land), each inside the others' intervals. The station, independent of any reanalysis, agrees with ERA5 on the day-to-day anomalies to 0.44\,$^\circ$C; the finer ERA5-Land agrees less well (0.56\,$^\circ$C), because its land-only tiles lose the sea's moderation at coastal sites. The lead halves with the station reading (joint $p = 0.11$), and ERA5's one weakness is the hottest days, which it reads 1.3\,$^\circ$C too cool.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Who responds}
One regression with the doses interacted with every site gives each site its own cold and heat slope; a random-effects decomposition separates the true spread between sites from sampling noise, and empirical-Bayes shrinkage gives each site a posterior slope for ranking and mapping. Sites without CHP, date effects included.

\begin{figblock}
\pair[0.9]{site_slopes_hdd14}{site_slopes_cdd20}
\captionof{figure}{Every site's cold (left) and heat (right) slope: the raw estimate with its 95\,\% interval in grey, the shrunk posterior as a point coloured by building use, sites ordered by posterior. Dashed lines at zero and at the pooled slope.}
\end{figblock}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The spread is real.} Both slopes vary far more between sites than their standard errors allow ($I^2 = 0.99$). The true between-site SD is 1.3 points for cold around a mean of $+1.1$\,\% per $^\circ$C, and 3.9 points for heat around $+1.0$: 82 sites rise significantly on cold days and 8 fall; 48 rise on hot days and 39 fall. ``The heat effect'' averages over sites that differ in sign, and a site's cold and heat slopes are only weakly related (correlation 0.2).
\item \textbf{Cold is about the heating fuel.} Building use moderates the cold slope (joint $p < 0.001$): against acute inpatient hospitals at $+0.05$\,\% per $^\circ$C, community inpatient, mental-health and non-inpatient sites add 1.2 to 1.4 points. The gas-to-electricity ratio is the one continuous moderator that survives jointly: one SD more gas removes 0.45 points ($p = 0.003$). Electricity responds to cold where heat is electric; a site that moves from gas to heat pumps should expect a cold slope of $+1$ to $+1.5$\,\% per $^\circ$C.
\item \textbf{Heat is unexplained.} None of the eight moderators (use, cluster, trust type, size, build year, climate, gas ratio, LED share) predicts the heat slope, alone or jointly. The large real dispersion rests on something the estates returns do not record, most plausibly whether a site has cooling plant and how it is controlled; that is the strongest case for asking trusts for plant data.
\item \textbf{By hour.} On cold days, non-inpatient, mental-health and community inpatient sites run 15 to 30\,\% above a 15\,$^\circ$C day through the whole day; the five acute hospitals rise 6 to 8\,\%, gas carrying their heating. On hot days, mental-health and non-inpatient sites are flat until midday and climb to $+12$ to $+15$\,\% at 17:00--19:00, the evening shift group by group.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What moves heat}
The heat slope is taken apart on the same panel with a site-specific sunshine term (ERA5 surface solar radiation), the demand of each six-hour block of the day and of the weekend, and the heat dose split into hot afternoons (daily maximum above 24\,$^\circ$C) and warm nights (daily minimum above 12\,$^\circ$C). Two open datasets join the moderators: the ONS rural-urban class and Census 2021 density of every site postcode, and the DESNZ Renewable Energy Planning Database; two ERIC fields not used before, overheating occurrences in clinical areas and solar consumed on site. Sites flagged for meter gaps are left out (24; they owned the tails of the previous section, Bridlington at $-15$\,\% per $^\circ$C among them), leaving 83.

\begin{figblock}
\pair[0.9]{heat_sunshine_control}{heat_night_day}
\captionof{figure}{Left: each site's heat slope against temperature alone and net of a site sunshine term; the daylight saving masks some cooling (pooled $+0.6 \to +1.1$\,\% per $^\circ$C) but leaves the spread, and 22 sites still fall with heat. Right: the dose split in two, per site: cooling plant lives in the upper right (hot afternoons, and warm nights too), the fallers in the lower left, responding as much to warm nights as to hot afternoons.}
\end{figblock}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sunshine is real but is not the heat story.} 55 of 83 sites import less on sunny days at a given temperature, $-0.9$\,\% per kWh/m$^2$ pooled ($-5$\,\% between an overcast and a clear summer day), with the shape of daylight ($-1.4$\,\% in the afternoon block, $+0.2$\,\% at night) and 1.9 points stronger where ERIC records solar generation. The heat and sunshine slopes of a site are unrelated ($r = 0.05$); REPD shows no rooftop solar operational at these sites within the sample (24 schemes, all 2025 applications awaiting construction).
\item \textbf{Heat is an afternoon phenomenon.} Pooled, $+0.2$\,\% per $^\circ$C at night, $+0.8$ in the morning, $+3.6$ in the afternoon, $+2.1$ in the evening; 32 of the 41 risers have an afternoon block significantly above their night block. Thirteen risers respond significantly less at weekends: plant that runs with the working day.
\item \textbf{The fallers are a heating tail, not a cooling behaviour.} Risers respond twice as much to hot afternoons as to warm nights (Table~\ref{tab:heat_decomposition}); fallers respond to both equally, and 17 of 22 have a significantly negative warm-night slope. They are small buildings (median 9\,kW against 21\,kW) whose heating, its auxiliaries and water heating are still running in the warm season and switch off when the night is warm: Kelling Hospital runs 50\,kW in winter and 19\,kW in summer. They carry 8\,\% of the demand.
\item \textbf{Location explains nothing; electrical intensity does.} Urban form (joint $p = 0.92$), population density ($p = 0.38$), building use ($p = 0.35$), build year, heating fuel, LED share: none. Peak demand per m$^2$ ($+0.7$ points per SD, $p = 0.05$), electricity intensity in the joint model ($+1.9$ per SD, $p = 0.001$), tenure ($p = 0.02$) and ERIC solar generation ($+2.0$, $p = 0.01$, a marker of trusts that invest in plant) do. A random-effects meta-regression gives the share of the true between-site variance each descriptor set explains: every estates and context set, nothing ($-8$ to $+6$\,\%); the site's own diurnal signature, 46\,\%. The heat slope is about plant on the site, visible in the shape of its day and absent from the estates return.
\item \textbf{Types.} 41 sites rise on hot afternoons (74\,\% of metered demand, 25 of them also falling with sunshine), 12 fall with sunshine only, 22 fall with heat, 8 neither; the types are mixed within every region.
\item \textbf{The Display Energy Certificates confirm the direction and stop short of the size.} Every DEC at the site postcodes was pulled through the MHCLG API (3,266 certificates, 285 current buildings; 67 of the 83 sites have one, certified floor area 1.08 times the ERIC area at the median). ``Air conditioning present'' is true at 51 of 67 sites and separates nothing ($+1.0$ point, $p = 0.46$). The building environment does: the three sites classed as air-conditioned have a heat slope 3.1 points above the naturally ventilated reference ($p < 0.001$), mixed mode 2.1\,\% per $^\circ$C against 1.0, and the cooled floor share adds 0.9 points per SD in the joint model ($p = 0.02$). The register classes most hospital buildings as heated and naturally ventilated while they rise on hot afternoons, and records the plant's size on 19\,\% of recent certificates, so the diurnal signature remains the better measure. The fallers' certificates name gas as the heating fuel: their warm-night fall is the auxiliaries and hot water of gas heating, not electric space heating. The 115 advisory reports linked to current certificates carry 1,552 recommendations (fabric 335, controls 322, heating 284, renewables 214, cooling 170), stored per site by theme as features for later work.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Causal machine learning}
On the same residualised panel, an R-learner and an econml causal forest, cross-fitted over site folds so no site's effect is predicted by a model that saw it, search the covariate space for the heat response (Figure~\ref{fig:cate}). The forest's out-of-fold site effects correlate 0.21 with the fixed-effect slopes and recover 24\,\% of their true between-site variance from observables alone, where the linear moderators recovered none; a placebo with shuffled covariates gives $-0.06$. The forest splits on the load shape (night/day and winter/summer ratios), size and the summer maximum, not on the estates or certificate fields; with the site's diurnal signature added, the daytime sunshine slope takes 44\,\% of the importance. Calendar modifiers (weekend, bank holiday, school summer) do not change the effect. The learned effects are over-dispersed (calibration slope 0.19) and the fallers sit near zero: their heating tail has nothing to split on.

\subsection{A learned site embedding}
A joint-embedding predictive architecture was trained on the half-hourly panel (28-day windows, one token per day, seven masked target days whose embeddings a predictor must produce from the rest; 0.48 million parameters) with a pre-registered test: does a site's embedding predict its heat slope out of fold better than the diurnal signature (46\,\%)? It does not: 1\,\% of the true between-site heat variance from the JEPA embedding, 0\,\% from the Chronos-2 encoder's embedding of the same demand, 7\,\% from the observables, 36\,\% from the signature (Figure~\ref{fig:jepa}). The same embeddings do carry the responses that show in the day's shape: Chronos-2 recovers 63\,\% of the sunshine slope and 42\,\% of the cold slope, where the observables manage 28 and 18. A weather-blind representation learns what a profile looks like, not how it responds to a driver it never sees; the next version conditions the tokens and the predictor on the day's weather. The latent surprise of a held-out day is the usable by-product: it spikes at the CHP hospitals where the changepoint search put steps and stays flat at small sites.
